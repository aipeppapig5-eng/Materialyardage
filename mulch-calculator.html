How many cubic yards of mulch do I need?

Mulch is sold two ways: in bags measured in cubic feet, and in bulk measured in cubic yards. Both start from the same calculation.

Multiply the length and width of the bed in feet to get the area in square feet. Multiply that by the depth in feet — the depth in inches divided by twelve — to get cubic feet. Divide by 27 to convert to cubic yards, since a cubic yard contains 27 cubic feet.

Cubic feet = length (ft) × width (ft) × (depth in ÷ 12) Cubic yards = cubic feet ÷ 27 Bags = cubic feet ÷ bag size in ft³

In metric units, multiply length and width in meters by the depth in centimeters divided by one hundred, which gives cubic meters directly.

Cubic meters = length (m) × width (m) × (depth cm ÷ 100)

How many bags of mulch do I need?

The most common bag size in North America is 2 cubic feet, and it takes 13.5 of them to make a cubic yard. Bags of 3 cubic feet are also widely sold — 9 make a cubic yard. Because you cannot buy a partial bag, always round up.

Mulch depth guide

Depth is the decision that most affects both cost and performance. Too thin and weeds push through and moisture escapes; too thick and water struggles to reach the soil while shallow roots suffocate.

Recommended mulch depths by situation.
SituationDepthWhy
Annual and perennial beds2 inRetains moisture without smothering small plants

2-inch, 3-inch and 4-inch examples

For the same 300 square foot bed, the depth you choose changes the order significantly:

At 2 in: 300 × (2 ÷ 12) = 50.0 ft³ = 1.85 yd³ = 25 bags (2 ft³) At 3 in: 300 × (3 ÷ 12) = 75.0 ft³ = 2.78 yd³ = 38 bags (2 ft³) At 4 in: 300 × (4 ÷ 12) = 100 ft³ = 3.70 yd³ = 50 bags (2 ft³)

Going from two inches to four doubles the material and doubles the cost. If budget is tight, three inches on the visible front beds and two inches elsewhere is a reasonable compromise.

Worked example

A 20 ft × 15 ft bed at 3 inches deep

Area = 20 × 15 = 300 sq ft Depth in feet = 3 ÷ 12 = 0.25 ft Cubic feet = 300 × 0.25 = 75 ft³ Cubic yards = 75 ÷ 27 = 2.78 yd³ Bags (2 ft³) = 75 ÷ 2 = 37.5 → 38 bags Bags via yards = 2.78 × 13.5 = 37.5 → 38 bags Est. weight = 2.78 × 0.4 = 1.11 tons

Both routes to the bag count agree, which is a useful check: dividing cubic feet by the bag size and multiplying cubic yards by 13.5 are the same calculation expressed differently.

How to measure garden beds

Beds are rarely rectangular, and the measuring method matters more than precision to the inch.

  • Curved front edges. Measure the length along the middle of the bed rather than along the outer curve, and take the width at three or four points to average out the bulges.
  • Beds around a house. Break them into straight sections at each corner and calculate each separately. Adding three rectangles is quicker than trying to average an L-shape.
  • Island beds. Treat an oval as 3.14 × half the length × half the width. A 12 ft × 8 ft oval is about 75 square feet, not the 96 you would get from treating it as a rectangle.
  • Tree rings. The area of a ring is 3.14 × (outer radius² − inner radius²). For a 6 ft diameter ring around a 1 ft trunk flare, that is about 25 square feet.
  • Deduct large plantings. Established shrubs with wide bases occupy real volume. On a densely planted bed, subtracting 10–15% of the area is usually about right.
If you are mulching several beds, calculate each one separately and add the cubic yards together before rounding. Rounding each bed up individually can leave you ordering a yard more than you need.

Bagged versus bulk mulch

Bagged mulch is clean to handle, easy to store and simple to buy a little at a time. Bulk mulch arrives loose by the cubic yard and normally carries a delivery fee plus a minimum order.

For larger projects, bulk delivery may be more economical than buying bags, depending on local material, delivery and bag prices. The comparison worth making is straightforward: multiply the bag price by 13.5 to get the cost of a cubic yard in bags, then compare that against the delivered price of one cubic yard including the delivery charge, divided across however many yards you are ordering.

Other factors beyond price:

  • Handling. A cubic yard is 13.5 bags to carry, or one pile to shovel and barrow. Which is easier depends on access and on whether you have a wheelbarrow.
  • Storage. Bags can sit in a garage for weeks. A bulk pile needs to be spread reasonably soon, especially in wet weather.
  • Consistency. Bulk mulch from a landscape supplier is often coarser and longer-lasting than the finely shredded product in bags.
  • Vehicle limits. A cubic yard of mulch is much lighter than soil or gravel, so a pickup can usually take two to three yards by weight — though volume becomes the constraint well before that.

Mulch types and their typical uses

Shredded hardwood bark is the default choice for most beds. It knits together, resists washing away on slopes, and breaks down over a season or two, adding organic matter to the soil. It is the most widely available bulk option.

Bark nuggets are larger pieces that last considerably longer before decomposing. They float in heavy rain, so they suit flat beds rather than slopes, and their coarse texture means weeds sometimes find gaps.

Wood chips are often available cheaply, sometimes free, from tree services. They are excellent on paths and around trees, though fresh chips can be quite variable and are better suited to established plantings than to newly seeded beds.

Pine straw is common in the southeastern United States. It is very light per cubic yard, acidifies the soil slightly as it breaks down, and is usually sold in bales rather than cubic yards.

Rubber mulch does not decompose, which makes it long-lasting but means it contributes nothing to the soil. It is mainly used for playground surfacing where impact absorption is the goal. It is significantly heavier than organic mulch per cubic yard.

Dyed mulch is usually shredded wood coloured black, brown or red. Colour fades over a year or so. It calculates identically to undyed shredded mulch.

Frequently asked questions

How many bags of mulch are in a cubic yard?

Thirteen and a half bags of 2 cubic feet, or nine bags of 3 cubic feet. A cubic yard is 27 cubic feet, so divide 27 by the bag size in cubic feet.

How much area does a 2 cubic foot bag cover?

About 12 square feet at 2 inches deep, 8 square feet at 3 inches, and 6 square feet at 4 inches.

How deep should mulch be?

Two to three inches works for most beds. Three to four inches suppresses weeds better around established shrubs. Keep mulch pulled back from trunks and stems rather than piled against them, as constant moisture against bark invites rot.

How much does a cubic yard of mulch weigh?

Bark and wood mulch is commonly estimated at roughly 0.3 to 0.5 tons per cubic yard when reasonably dry. Saturated mulch can weigh considerably more, which is worth remembering if you are hauling it yourself.

Do I remove old mulch before adding new?

Usually not. Organic mulch decomposes into the soil, so topping up to the original depth is normal practice. If layers have built up beyond three or four inches over several years, raking some out before adding more is worthwhile.

How often does mulch need replacing?

Most organic mulch needs topping up annually or every other year, depending on climate and how finely shredded it is. Coarse nuggets last longer; fine shredded bark breaks down faster.

Should I order extra?

Adding 5 to 10 percent covers uneven ground and settling. Bag purchases are easier to adjust than a bulk delivery, so it is more important to allow a margin when ordering in bulk.
